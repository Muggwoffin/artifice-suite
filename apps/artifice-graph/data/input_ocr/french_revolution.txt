The French Revolution, beginning in 1789, fundamentally transformed European political
thought. King Louis XVI of France convened the Estates-General to address the kingdom's
financial crisis. The Third Estate, led by figures such as Maximilien Robespierre and
Jean-Paul Marat, declared itself the National Assembly.

The storming of the Bastille on July 14, 1789, became a powerful symbol of the revolution.
The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, adopted in August 1789, drew
heavily on Enlightenment philosophy and the writings of John Locke and Jean-Jacques
Rousseau.

The Reign of Terror (1793-1794), orchestrated by Robespierre through the Committee of
Public Safety, saw the execution of thousands by guillotine, including King Louis XVI
and Marie Antoinette. The Committee of Public Safety, dominated by Robespierre, wielded
near-dictatorial power.

Napoleon Bonaparte, a military genius, rose through the ranks during the Revolutionary
Wars. His coup d'état of 18 Brumaire (November 1799) effectively ended the Revolution
and established the Consulate. Napoleon later crowned himself Emperor in 1804, marking
the transition from the Revolutionary period to the Napoleonic era.

The Revolution's ideals of liberty, equality, and fraternity influenced subsequent
movements across Europe and the Americas. Thomas Paine, the English-born philosopher,
advocated for revolutionary ideals in works such as "Rights of Man" (1791).
