On May 8th I met Retzlaw, alias Karl Groehl, at No 3 Regent Square, where our mutual friend Fritz Gross resides. Retzlaw was not long in asking me what I thought of his circular, of which I have already forwarded a copy. He told me he had sent out 300 of these circulars to various emigrants and had received most of these contained adverse criticism. Some of the objectors wanted to preserve a small but efficient German army to defend the new German state against capitalist aggressors. Retzlaw said this would be the same old story as the Reichswehr over again. Others objected to the idea of Germany having to make reparations or to the admission of German war guilt and the punishment of war criminals. A further series protested against the idea of abolishing the official church; among these latter was Strasser, who intimated that he could not exist without the spiritual comfort the church could give. Retzlaw wants to gather together a body of emigrants who would prepare themselves for the mission of running postwar Germany. In his opinion we are now well in the third quarter of the war; the end may come sooner than we expect, and if we are not ready to take over Germany when it does, nationalistic elements in Germany will provide themselves with the means for an underground existence until they can come into the open and start another war. As regards the administration of Germany, for instance, we cannot leave the Nazi-appointed officials to carry on because they will conduct anti-allied propaganda, and yet we cannot dispense with them unless we have people ready to take their places. We cannot allow German schools to give instruction from Nazi text books, and yet we cannot close the German schools altogether, which we should have to do unless we have the textbooks ready for the use of their pupils. But these textbooks must be written and printed, and that takes time; ergo, let a suitable body of emigrants get on with the job right away. The end of the war will find Europe empty of the barest necessities of life; many houses will be without beds, tables or chairs. Some of these will have to be made; others will have to be taken from Germany. This task can only be done by anti-Nazi emigrants who would know where to look for articles that have to be confiscated for the benefit of the plundered European lands. Groehl thinks he can get the people, but was somewhat vague when I tried to ascertain what type of emigrants he had in mind, especially as he had not a good word for any of the German parties carrying on in London. Gross was equally vague when I asked him later what support he thought Retzlaw would find, saying that anarchists and Trotskyists like Retzlaw and himself could not expect to be popular, but that he liked Retzlaw because he was at least sincere. Retzlaw would like to run a German paper in London which would not give current news but serve as a discussion forum for postwar reconstruction in Germany. He said he had recently run across Fritz Wolf and mentioned the matter to him. Wolf was sympathetic and said that he was wanting to start a German paper in London himself and that he could put up the money for one, but that he must talk first to Vogel and Ollenhauer because they would be associated in the venture.
