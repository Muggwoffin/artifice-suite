He said he had been asked by many people whether he thought Schiff was a Reichswehr agent, to which he replied that he would not go as far as that but that he thought Schiff was playing into the hands of the Reichswehr. He added that Stampfer was the same type, but rather worse.
He pointed out that in his circular he had let the Communist Party off rather lightly. But he could not attack all round; he had to spare someone.
The subject of Hitler's recent speech came up, and Retzlaw said he had analysed it very carefully. In his opinion there was no need for Hitler to threaten the various categories of persons he had mentioned, because he already had them under his thumb. But what category had Hitler never mention? The women, and it was these of whom he was really most afraid. Women were more elemental and less submissive than men. Hitler had promised the women of Germany husbands, homes and decent living conditions, but as usual, he had broken his promises and the German women were now seeing through him. Therefore the B.B.C. should make a greater special appeal to them. In any case the B.B.C.'s German propaganda was bad, and its greatest mistake was letting people like Ollenhauer go on the air. The Germans were fed up with the old gangs of all parties and did not want to hear them.
Retzlaw said he wanted to get his circular translated, so that he could send copies to prominent Britons. He thought of trying to get Gollancz to take it up, or, failing him, to put it at the disposal of the Fabian Society. The subject of Vansittart then cropped up. Retzlaw said he was not a Vansittartist, but that he considered Vansittart was quite right in his attitude. One had to be onesided in war; showing subsequent consideration to a beaten enemy was quite a different matter. One great advantage about Vansittart was that he had the Conservative Party behind him; many Conservatives had formerly been pro-Germans but Vansittart had converted them.
I could, of course, see by then that the real reason why Retzlaw wanted to meet me was that he thought he might induce me to translate his circular free of charge. I asked Gross about this after Retzlaw had left; he admitted that Retzlaw would certainly like me to translate it but that he would be too shy to ask me straight off. I replied that I was naturally very interested but also very full up with work, and that if I undertook to do it, I could only do so in my spare time, so that it might be rather a long while before I got it finished, and that he had better put that point of view to Retzlaw. He agreed to do so, adding that perhaps Retzlaw might have some money and want me to do it as a commercial proposition. Anyhow, he will put the case to Retzlaw and let me know later.

C.W. Sykes
E.S. 11/5/42
