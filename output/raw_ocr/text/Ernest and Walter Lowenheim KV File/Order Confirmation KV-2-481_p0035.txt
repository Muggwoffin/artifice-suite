4th May 1942.

Dear Mr. Sykes,

I am sorry to be so late with this report, but I have a touch of flu for some days now which impedes my action a bit.

KARTHAUSER'S information enables me to make the following additions to my previous reports on LOEWENHEIM (of 18/12/41 and 5/1/42). At the outset I would say that only WALTER L. is of interest, since his younger brother is nothing but a tool in his elder brother's hands and follows him blindly.

According to K. Loewenheim is a political man through and through. As early as 1918 he became involved in politics and started his career as a politician in a Socialist Youth Movement. Later on, he rose quickly in the ranks of the Communists and I understand that at the age of 25 he was one of their prominent leaders. He was chosen to represent the German Communist Party at the 7th World Congress in Russia, he led in person the negotiations with LENIN and he became an honorary member of the Red Military Academy. As reported previously, he had later on to leave the Communist Party, but I am doubtful whether the date I gave for this event, namely 1921, is quite correct.

After his severance from the Communists L. continued his political activities as a left-wing Socialist. He became a member of "Neubeginnen" and, after the Nazis got into power, he did valuable underground-work against them. The development in Germany convinced him, however, that the methods of "Neubeginnen" in fighting the Nazis did not have results to justify their great cost in manpower which fell victim to the Gestapo. L. therefore left "Neubeginnen". At present he is as keen as ever on political work and he devotes almost all his time to it, but according to K. he has lost most of his influence and importance, at least as far as current politics are concerned. His chief plans now relate to post-war construction.

K. gave me the following account of L's. character and personality. L. had a brilliant brain and a flair for politics. As far back as 1929 he had, for instance, written a memorandum predicting the assumption of power by the Nazis. At a time when many of his Party-friends did not realise this danger, he had tried to prevent his fears from coming true and for this purpose he had made all possible efforts to create a united German Labour Front. L. was also very clever in working out schemes and intrigues, but he often made the same mistake, namely he failed to pay sufficient attention to the human side of political affairs. His plans were sound in theory, but they were usually spoilt by the personal reactions of the people involved. He was a bad judge of persons.

K. believes that the reason for this lack of judgment - surprising in a man of L's. intelligence - is to be found in L's. own character. L. has, probably owing to his unattractive, if not revolting appearance an inferiority complex. This leads him to use whenever possible a substitute for the purpose of putting into practice the schemes he has worked out. He does not like to come into the open himself. Such technique is completely in line with the rest of his character (which K. pictures as dishonest, selfish and unscrupulous) since it allows him not only to get better results by using persons of a more pleasant personality than himself, but also to use his substitutes as scapegoats
